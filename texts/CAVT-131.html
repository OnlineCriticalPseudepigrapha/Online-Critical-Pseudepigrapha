2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum (commonly abbreviated LAB, also known as Pseudo-Philo or Biblical Antiquities) is one of the most substantial and significant works of Second Temple Jewish literature. Spanning sixty-five chapters, the work is a monumental "Rewritten Bible" narrative recounting sacred history from Adam to the tragic death of King Saul. Pseudepigraphically ascribed to the 1st-century Jewish-Alexandrian philosopher Philo Judaeus—having survived in medieval Latin manuscripts alongside Latin translations of genuine Philonic treatises—the work was decisively reclaimed for Jewish scholarship by Leopold Cohn in 1898.

Scholarly consensus demonstrates that the extant Latin text was translated from a lost Greek intermediary, which itself was translated from an authentic Palestinian Hebrew original composed in the late 1st century CE (ca. 70–135 CE, or shortly before the fall of Jerusalem).

Architectural Breakdown (65 Chapters)

1. Primeval History: From Adam to the Tower of Babel (chs. 1–7):

  • Extensive genealogies enumerating descendants unmentioned in Genesis (sons and daughters of Adam, Seth, and Cain).
  • The Flood and the repopulation of the earth under Noah’s sons.
  • The Rebellion of Babel and Nimrod (chs. 5–7): Nimrod and the leaders of the earth build the Tower of Babel. Abraham, along with eleven righteous men, refuses to contribute bricks; Abraham is cast into a blazing furnace, but God sends the angel of fire (Zeruel) to rescue him unharmed, while the flames consume 84,000 onlookers.

2. The Patriarchs, Egypt, and the Exodus (chs. 8–10):

  • Covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
  • Amram's Resistance and Miriam’s Prophecy (ch. 9): Facing Pharaoh’s decree to drown male infants, Amram leads the elders of Israel in refusing celibacy, trusting in divine covenant fidelity; Miriam receives an angelic night vision foretelling that her brother Moses will save Israel.
  • Moses’ birth, preservation by Pharaoh’s daughter Thermuthis, the Plagues, and the miraculous crossing of the Red Sea.

3. The Theophany at Sinai and the Law (chs. 11–14):

  • Cosmic Sinai theophany: heaven bows down, the deeps tremble, and celestial angels tremble at the voice of God.
  • Promulgation of the Decalogue and statutory laws; the Golden Calf apostasy; Moses intercedes, smashes the tablets, and reconstructs the Tabernacle.

4. The Wilderness Wandering and the Death of Moses (chs. 15–19):

  • The twelve spies; the rebellion and subterranean destruction of Korah; the miraculous budding of Aaron’s rod.
  • Moses' Farewell and Heavenly Vision (ch. 19): Before dying, Moses is shown the celestial sanctuary, the measurements of the cosmos, the path of the stars, and the storehouses of souls; God Himself buries Moses on the high mountain in heavenly light, weeping over His servant while angels lament.

5. Joshua and the Conquest of the Land (chs. 20–24):

  • Joshua succeeds Moses; Rahab’s faith; crossing the Jordan; destruction of Jericho and Ai; renewal of the covenant at Shechem; Joshua's testament and death.

6. The Great Kenaz Epic: Judge and Deliverer (chs. 25–28):

  • The centerpiece of LAB's narrative innovation, transforming the minor biblical figure Kenaz (Judg 3:9–11) into an epic national hero:
  • Kenaz uncovers hidden idolatrous cults within the twelve tribes, casting lots and extracting confessions.
  • He receives twelve luminous celestial stones from Mount Hermon that shine in darkness and liquefy idols like water.
  • The Nocturnal Battle: Armed with divine courage and accompanied by angels (Ingethel and Zeruel) who strike the Philistines with blindness and numbness, Kenaz rides alone into the enemy camp and slaughters 100,000 Philistines in a single night.
  • Kenaz experiences an apocalyptic vision of the creation of the heavens, the firmament, and the final judgment before dying peacefully.

7. Zebul, Deborah, and the Victory over Sisera (chs. 29–33):

  • Zebul rules as righteous judge.
  • Deborah arises as prophetess, judge, and mother in Israel; her victory song over Sisera; Jael slays Sisera.
  • The Death of Deborah: A poignant testamentary farewell where the people weep and Deborah instructs them to set their hearts upon the Law, for after death no intercession is possible (ch. 33).

8. Gideon, Abimelech, and the Tragedy of Seila (chs. 34–41):

  • Gideon’s defeat of Midian; Abimelech’s murderous reign; Jair’s idolatrous shrine.
  • Jephthah and Seila (chs. 39–40): Jephthah vows to sacrifice whatever meets him first; his daughter Seila (Seila filia Iephte) voluntarily accepts her sacrificial death, asking for two months to wander the mountains.
  • The Lament of Seila: A sublime poetic dirge lamenting her unwed bridal chamber, weeping among the hills, and embracing her death as an offering to God.

9. Samson and the End of the Judges (chs. 42–47):

  • Manoah and his barren wife Eluma; the angelic annunciation; Samson's supernatural exploits and self-sacrifice against the Philistines.
  • The idol of Micah; the atrocity of the concubine of Gibeah and the war against Benjamin; Phinehas the high priest intercedes and is assumed to heaven to reappear as Elijah (ch. 48).

10. Eli, Samuel, and the Tragedy of King Saul (chs. 48–65):

  • Hannah’s prophetic song; birth and divine calling of Samuel; defeat of the Philistines and recovery of the Ark.
  • Israel requests an earthly king; Samuel anoints Saul.
  • Saul’s disobedience regarding Agag and Amalek; David anointed by Samuel; David sings mystical exorcism hymns to soothe Saul’s tormenting evil spirit (ch. 60).
  • Saul consults the Witch of Endor; Samuel rises from Sheol and pronounces irrevocable doom upon Saul.
  • Mount Gilboa: Saul commits suicide, requesting an Amalekite to dispatch him; David laments Saul and Jonathan, and is acclaimed king over Israel (ch. 65).

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum has been preserved through a remarkably cohesive Latin manuscript tradition originating in medieval German and Austrian Benedictine and Cistercian monasteries.

A. The Latin Manuscript Tradition

Extant in eighteen complete and fragmentary codices (11th–15th centuries), categorized into two primary stemmatic branches ($\Pi$ and $\Delta$) by Guido Kisch, Daniel J. Harrington, and Howard Jacobson:

1. Primary Witnesses:

  • MS A (Admont, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. 359): 11th century; the oldest, purest, and most authoritative extant manuscript of LAB.
  • MS C (Fulda / Kassel, Murhardsche Bibliothek, Cod. 4° Theol. 6): 11th century; closely allied with MS A.
  • MS D (Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 17133): 12th century; from the monastery of Schäftlarn.
  • MS B (Basel, Universitätsbibliothek, Cod. F. III. 15b): 15th century.
  • MS E (Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. 4460): 15th century.
  • MS F (Melk, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. 1477): 15th century.
  • MS G (Graz, Universitätsbibliothek, Cod. 749): 14th century.
  • Fragment 63 (Freiburg im Breisgau, Universitätsbibliothek): 12th-century fragment preserving portions of chapters 26–28; identified and edited by F. Petit and V. Sack (1983).

B. The Editio Princeps (1527)

  • Johannes Sichardus (Sichardt) published the editio princeps in Basel (Philonis Iudaei Alexandrini libri antiquitatum, apud Henricum Petrum, August 1527).
  • Sichardus utilized an ancient manuscript (now lost) belonging to the abbey of Lorsch or Fulda, which represented an independent textual witness often preserving superior readings over surviving codices.

C. Medieval Hebrew Retroversions: The Chronicles of Jerahmeel

  • Preserved in Oxford Bodleian MS Heb. d. 11 (fols. 34r–58r), a 14th-century compilation assembled by Eleazar ben Asher ha-Levi incorporating texts from Jerahmeel ben Solomon (12th c., northern Italy/southern France).
  • The Hebrew Portions: Chapters 1–8 (Babel, Abraham in the furnace) and 25–28 (the Kenaz cycle) survive in Hebrew in the Chronicles of Jerahmeel.
  • Critical Status: Moses Gaster (1896, 1899) initially claimed that Jerahmeel preserved the lost Semitic original of LAB. However, Adolf Neubauer (1887), Leopold Cohn (1915), Daniel J. Harrington (1974), and Howard Jacobson (1983, 1997) proved decisively that Jerahmeel's Hebrew text is a medieval Hebrew translation made directly from a Latin manuscript of LAB.
