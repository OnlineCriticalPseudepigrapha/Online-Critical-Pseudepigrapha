2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs is an extensive pseudepigraphon consisting of twelve farewell discourses delivered on their deathbeds by the twelve sons of Jacob (Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Joseph, and Benjamin) to their assembled children.

Structure of the Individual Testaments

Each testament follows a consistent, threefold tripartite literary pattern:

1. Autobiographical / Haggadic Narrative: The patriarch recounts key episodes from his life (expanding upon Genesis), candidly confessing his primary sin (e.g., Reuben's lust, Simeon's envy, Judah's drunkenness and greed, Gad's hatred) or extolling his characteristic virtue (e.g., Issachar's simplicity, Zebulun's compassion, Joseph's self-control/chastity).

2. Moral Exhortation (Paraenesis): The patriarch warns his descendants against the spiritual perils of vice (especially porneia, anger, jealousy, and double-heartedness) and exhorts them to practice righteousness, single-minded devotion (haplotēs), and fraternal love.

3. Eschatological Prophecy and Farewell: The patriarch predicts the future apostasy, exile, and restoration of his tribe—frequently structured around the Sin-Exile-Return pattern—culminating in an apocalyptic vision of the coming Messiah(s) from the tribes of Levi and Judah, a final resurrection, and instructions for burial in the ancestral tomb at Hebron (Cave of Machpelah).

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

A. Greek Textual Tradition

The complete Greek text survives in 14 principal manuscripts. R. H. Charles initially classified the tradition into two recensions, $\alpha$ (MSS c, h, i) and $\beta$ (MSS a, b, d, e, f, g, j). The modern editio maior (M. de Jonge, H. J. de Jonge, H. W. Hollander, T. Korteweg) replaced this with a division into Family I (MSS b and k) and Family II (all other Greek MSS, alongside the Armenian and Slavonic versions):

  • b: Cambridge University Library, MS Ff. 1.24, fols. 203a–262b (late 10th c.; brought from Athens to England by Robert Grosseteste in the 13th c.; the base text of Sinker and de Jonge's early editions).
  • k: Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, cod. Gr. Z. 494 (coll. 331), fols. 263r–264v (mid-13th c.; preserves large extracts).
  • a: Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Barocci 133, fols. 278r–309r (ca. 1270 CE).
  • c: Vatican, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, cod. Vat. gr. 731, fols. 97r–167r (13th c.).
  • d: Vatican, cod. Vat. gr. 1238, fols. 350r–380v (late 12th c.; includes significant marginal variants and scholia).
  • e: Mount Athos, Monastery of Koutloumousiou, cod. 39, fols. 180r–227r (11th c.; celebrated for containing inserted Greek translations of large sections of the Aramaic Levi Document).
  • f: Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, cod. gr. 2658, fols. 41r–100v (11th c.).
  • g: Patmos, Monastery of St. John the Theologian, cod. 412, fols. 96r–148v (15th c.).
  • h: Sinai, Saint Catherine's Monastery, cod. gr. 547, fols. 195r–265v (17th c.).
  • i: Sinai, cod. gr. 548 (17th c.).
  • j: Sinai, cod. gr. 2170 (18th c.).
  • l: Mount Athos, Great Lavra, MS I 48, fols. 204r–276r (16th–17th c.; discovered and published by C. Burchard, 1966).
  • m: Ankara, Türk Tarih Kurumu, MS gr. 60, pp. 339–483 (16th c.).
  • n: Mount Athos, Monastery of Vatopedi, cod. 659 (14th c.).

B. Semitic Forebears and Witnesses (Genizah and Qumran)

1. The Aramaic Levi Document (ALD):

  • Cairo Genizah: Cambridge University Library MS T-S 16.94 (10th/11th c., cols. a–f); Oxford Bodleian MS heb. c 27, fol. 56 (10th/11th c., cols. a–d).
  • Qumran Cave 1: 1Q21 (1QTLevi; 60 fragments).
  • Qumran Cave 4: 4Q213 (4QLeviᵃ ar), 4Q213a (4QLeviᵇ ar), 4Q213b (4QLeviᶜ ar), 4Q214 (4QLeviᵈ ar), 4Q214a (4QLeviᵉ ar), 4Q214b (4QLeviᶠ ar), dated to the 2nd–1st c. BCE.
  • Greek Athos Additions: Mount Athos Koutloumousiou MS 39 preserves Greek interpolations representing a direct translation of the missing sections of ALD (prayer of Levi, priestly investiture, ritual halakhah on wood and sacrificial offerings).
  • Syriac Fragment: London, British Library Add. MS 14668, fol. 1 (6th/7th c.).

2. Hebrew Testament of Naphtali:

  • Qumran Cave 4: 4Q215 (4QTNaphtali; 1st c. BCE), preserving a genealogy of Bilhah, Jacob's prayer, and an eschatological column.
  • Medieval Hebrew Recension: Preserved in Oxford Bodleian MS Heb. d. 11 and incorporated into Moses Gaster's edition of the Chronicles of Jerahmeel (ch. 33).

3. Proto-Judah and Proto-Joseph Scrolls:

  • 3Q7 (3QTJud?); 4Q538 (4QTJud ar); 4Q539 (4QTJos ar).

C. Ancient Versions

  • Armenian Version: Survives in at least 48 manuscripts (oldest dated 1220 CE, Venice Mechitarist MSS 345, 1005, 1006; Jerusalem Armenian Patriarchate MSS 1925, 1927). Represents a distinct early translation (Family II), frequently preserving shorter, non-interpolated readings (studied by F. C. Conybeare, C. Burchard, and A. Hultgård).
  • Slavonic Versions: Widespread Old Church Slavonic and Serbian redactions preserved in the Tolkovaja Paleja and chronographic miscellanies (studied by A. Iatsimirskii, I. Franko, H. E. Gaylord, and T. Korteweg).
  • Latin Version: Translated from Greek MS b (Cambridge Ff. 1.24) by Bishop Robert Grosseteste of Lincoln ca. 1242 CE; survived in over 80 medieval manuscripts and exercised immense influence across Western Christendom.
  • Syriac and Coptic: Fragmentary liturgical citations and allusions.
