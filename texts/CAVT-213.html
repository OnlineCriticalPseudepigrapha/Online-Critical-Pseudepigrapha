2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Lives of the Prophets (Greek: Περὶ τῶν βίων καὶ τῆς τελευτῆς τῶν προφητῶν / Peri tōn biōn kai tēs teleutēs tōn prophētōn; Latin: Vitae prophetarum / De vitis prophetarum; Syriac: Tash'yāthā d-nebīyē) is an ancient Jewish hagiographical and biographical compendium detailing the careers, origins, ancestral tribes, prophetic signs, deaths, martyrdoms, and burial locations of twenty-three biblical prophets. Attributed spuriously in early Christian transmission to Epiphanius of Salamis (Cyprus, 4th c.) or Dorotheus of Tyre, the core document is a product of 1st-century CE Palestinian Judaism, compiled in Greek (or translated very early from Hebrew) prior to the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE.

The work is organized systematically by individual prophetic entries, typically following a rigid fourfold formula:

1. The prophet's hometown, geographical region, and ancestral tribe.

2. Distinctive miraculous deeds, prophetic oracles, and biographical anecdotes unrecorded in the biblical canon.

3. The circumstances of the prophet’s death (frequently violent martyrdom at the hands of wicked rulers or rebellious compatriots).

4. The exact topographical site of the prophet’s tomb and burial shrine, often accompanied by accounts of local pilgrimage, healing waters, or eschatological portents.

Survey of the Twenty-Three Prophetic Lives

A. The Four Major Prophets (chs. 1–4)

1. Isaiah (ch. 1): From Jerusalem; of the tribe of Judah; martyred under King Manasseh by being sawn in two with a wooden saw (priōni xylinō) beneath the Rogel oak tree. Before dying, Isaiah prayed for water, and God miraculously opened the spring of Siloam (Silōam, "Sent"); the spring flowed or ceased according to the prayer of the prophet. Buried near the Tomb of the Kings.

2. Jeremiah (ch. 2): From Anathoth; of the tribe of Levi; stoned to death by his own people at Taphnas (Tahpanhes) in Egypt and buried near Pharaoh’s palace. By prayer, Jeremiah banished the asp-vipers, crocodiles, and water-beasts (kēdoi) of Egypt. Before the fall of Jerusalem, Jeremiah miraculously concealed the Ark of the Covenant, the golden altar of incense, and the tables of stone inside a rock on Mount Nebo, sealing the crevice with his finger; the cloud of glory will not reveal the holy relics until the Lord gathers His dispersed people at the End of Days.

3. Ezekiel (ch. 3): From the land of Sarira; of the priestly order; slain in Chaldea by the leader of the Jewish exiles whom he reproved for practicing idolatry; buried in the double cave (spēlaion diploun) of Arphaxad. Ezekiel parted the waters of the river Chebar so fleeing exiles could escape the Chaldeans, and miraculously multiplied fish in the river during severe famine.

4. Daniel (ch. 4): From Upper Beth-horon; of the tribe of Judah; a man of renowned asceticism whose face shone with celestial beauty because he abstained from meat and wine. Interceded for Nebuchadnezzar during his zoomorphic madness, weeping for the beast-king until his reason returned. Buried in the royal tomb in Babylon.

B. The Twelve Minor Prophets (chs. 5–16)

5. Hosea (ch. 5): From Belemoth of the tribe of Issachar; gave the prophetic sign that the Lord would arrive on earth when the oak at Shiloh divided into twelve parts; buried in his native district.

6. Micah (ch. 6): From Morasthi of the tribe of Ephraim; executed by Joram son of Ahab (thrown from a precipice); buried on his family land near the burial ground of the Enakim.

7. Amos (ch. 7): From Tekoa; assaulted by Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, whose son Amaziah struck Amos’s skull with a club; carried half-dead back to Tekoa where he expired; buried with his ancestors.

8. Joel (ch. 8): From the territory of Reuben (Bethom); died in peace and buried in his home field.

9. Obadiah (ch. 9): From Beth-acharam; identified as the royal steward of Ahab who hid and fed one hundred prophets in a cave (1 Kgs 18:4); became a disciple of Elijah; buried in Samaria.

10. Jonah (ch. 10): From the district of Kariathmaus near Azotus; identified as the son of the widow of Zarephath whom Elijah revived from the dead (1 Kgs 17); after his mission to Nineveh, wandered with his mother to Sour (Tyre) and died; buried in the cave of Kenaz, the first judge.

11. Nahum (ch. 11): From Elkesi; foretold the overthrow of Nineveh by freshwater flood and subterranean fire; buried in his native land.

12. Habakkuk (ch. 12): From Beth-zouchar of the tribe of Simeon; fled to Ostrakine when Nebuchadnezzar advanced; carried by an angel by the hair of his head to Babylon to bring food to Daniel in the lions' den; gave the eschatological sign: "When a light shines in the Temple, they will see the glory of God"; buried in his own field.

13. Zephaniah (ch. 13): From the mountain of Sarabatha of the tribe of Simeon; prophesied concerning the captivity; buried in his field.

14. Haggai (ch. 14): Born in Babylon as a youth; saw the rebuilding of the Second Temple; buried near the tombs of the priests in Jerusalem.

15. Zechariah (ch. 15): Son of Berechiah; returned from Chaldea in extreme old age; blessed Zerubbabel and Josedek; buried beside Haggai.

16. Malachi (ch. 16): From Sopha; of the tribe of Zebulun; a man of exceptional piety and beauty; an angel of God appeared invisible to the multitude, repeating and confirming Malachi's prophecies (whence his name Angelos / Malachi); died young and buried in his ancestral tomb.

C. Prophets from the Historical Books (chs. 17–23)

17. Nathan (ch. 17): From Gaba; of the tribe of Judah; prevented David from building the Temple; died in old age and buried in his home.

18. Ahijah the Shilonite (ch. 18): From Shiloh; foretold the division of the kingdom to Jeroboam; saw a vision of two yoked heifers treading down the sanctuary; buried near the oak of Shiloh.

19. Joad (The Man of God from Judah) (ch. 19): From Samareim; the unnamed prophet of 1 Kings 13 slain by a lion after being deceived by the old prophet of Bethel; buried in Bethel beside his deceiver.

20. Azariah son of Oded (ch. 20): From the district of Subatha; urged Asa to purify Judah from idols; buried in his native field.

21. Elijah the Tishbite (ch. 21): From Thisbe of Gilead; of the priestly line; at his birth, his father Sobacha saw angels wrapping the newborn in bands of fire and feeding him with flames; ascended into heaven in a fiery chariot.

22. Elisha (ch. 22): From Abel-meholah of the tribe of Reuben; at his birth in Gilgal, the golden calf in Bethel bellowed so loudly it was heard throughout Jerusalem; buried in Samaria.

23. Zechariah son of Jehoiada (ch. 23): Slain by King Joash between the Temple altar and the sanctuary; his blood congealed upon the stone paving of the Temple and could not be washed away until the Babylonian invasion; buried in his father’s tomb in Jerusalem.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The Lives of the Prophets was among the most widely circulated hagiographical texts of late antiquity and the Byzantine Middle Ages, preserved across Greek, Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Arabic linguistic streams.

A. The Greek Manuscript Tradition

The Greek text is preserved in dozens of manuscripts exhibiting six major recensional families (classified by T. Schermann, E. Nestle, and J.-C. Haelewyck):

1. Epiphanii Recensio Prior (The Anonymous/Epiphanian Primitive Recension):

  • Preserved in Codex Marchalianus (Vat. gr. 2125) (6th/7th c., margin notes), Codex Parisinus gr. 1115 (dated 1276 CE), and Codex Vaticanus gr. 2024.
  • Considered by Torrey, Hare, and Schwemer to preserve the oldest and purest Jewish textual form.

2. Dorothei Recensio (Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre):

  • Found in historical catenae and discipular catalogs (Index discipulorum); characterized by stylistic smoothing and Byzantine chronographic notes.

3. Epiphanii Recensio Altera (Secondary Epiphanian Recension):

  • Represented by Codex Coislinianus 224 (10th/11th c.) and Codex Parisinus gr. 2496.

4. Recensio Anonyma:

  • Independent text form preserved without patristic attribution in Byzantine florilegia.

5. Recensio Scholiis Adiecta:

  • Integrated into the catenae and scholia of medieval Septuagint Bibles.

6. Recensio Liturgica (The Byzantine Synaxaria and Menologia):

  • Prophetic lives adapted into liturgical reading lessons for saints' feast days in the imperial Menologion of Basil II and the Great Synaxarion of Constantinople (ed. F. Halkin, A. Negoitǎ).

B. The Latin Tradition

  • Circulated early in the Western Church under the titles De vita et obitu prophetarum and De ortu et obitu patrum:
  • Isidore of Seville (De ortu et obitu patrum, 7th c., PL 83:129–156): Drew directly upon an early Latin translation of the Vitae.
  • Moses of Bergamo (12th c.): Translated Greek prophetic lives into Latin.
  • Critical studies and text editions by François Dolbeau (1986, 1990, 1994) recovered late antique Latin translations predating Isidore.

C. The Syriac Tradition

  • Widely diffused throughout the East Syrian (Nestorian) and West Syrian (Jacobite) churches:
  • Berlin MS Sachau 131 (fols. 57r–65v): ed. F. Baethgen (1886).
  • Union Theological Seminary MS Syr. 16: cataloged by M. Goshen-Gottstein (1979).
  • Solomon of Basra (The Book of the Bee, 13th c.): Incorporates chapters of the Vitae into chs. 32–33 (ed. E. A. W. Budge, Oxford, 1886, pp. 69–79).
  • Isho‘dad of Merv (9th c.): Extensively quotes the Vitae in his biblical commentaries (ed. C. van den Eynde, CSCO, 1962–1972).
  • Syriac chrestomathies: Eberhard Nestle, Syrische Grammatik (Berlin, 1888, pp. 86–107).

D. Other Ancient Versions

  • Ethiopic (Ge'ez): Translated from Christian Arabic or Greek; ed. J. Bachmann, Aethiopische Lesestücke (Leipzig, 1893); M. A. Knibb, "The Ethiopic Version of the Lives of the Prophets: Ezekiel and Daniel" (1985).
  • Armenian: Preserved in Armenian biblical manuscripts and Menologia; ed. S. Josepheanz (Ankanon girk‘, Venice, 1896, pp. 207–227); trans. J. Issaverdens (Venice, 1901, pp. 141–168).
  • Arabic: Extant in numerous Christian Arabic codices (Sinai Ar. 389, 448); ed. Oscar Löfgren, "An Arabic Version of the 'Vitae prophetarum'" (Orientalia Suecana 25–26 [1976–1977]: 77–105).
