2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Prayer of Joseph (Oratio Josephi / Προσευχὴ Ἰωσήφ) is an enigmatic Jewish-Hellenistic visionary work composed in Greek between the late 1st century BCE and the early 2nd century CE (ca. 70–130 CE), almost certainly in Roman Egypt or Syria. According to the Byzantine canon lists (Stichometry of Nicephorus, Synopsis of Pseudo-Athanasius, List of Sixty Books), the complete work originally comprised 1,100 stichoi (lines). Although the continuous text has perished, three extensive fragments survive as quotations in the theological treatises of Origen of Alexandria:

The Surviving Fragments

1. Fragment A (apud Origen, Commentarius in Johannem 2.31.188–190):

  • The patriarch Jacob speaks in the first person, revealing his primordial celestial identity:

> "I, Jacob, whom men call Jacob, but whose name is Israel, am an angel of God and a principal power (archikē dynamis), and Abraham and Isaac were created before every work. But I, Jacob, whom men call Jacob, my name is Israel, whom God called Israel, 'a man seeing God' (anēr horōn theon), because I am the firstborn of every creature (prōtogonos pantos zōou) animated by God!"

  • Jacob describes his descent from the heavenly realm to earthly embodiment and his confrontation with the archangel Uriel at the river Jabbok (Gen 32:24–30):

> "And when I was coming from Syrian Mesopotamia, Uriel the angel of God came forth and said: 'I have descended to earth and tabernacled among men, and I am called by the name of Jacob.' He was envious of me and wrestled with me. And I told him his name and what rank he holds among the sons of God: 'Are you not Uriel, the eighth after me? And am I not Israel, an archangel of the power of the Lord and a chief captain among the sons of God? Am I not Israel, the first minister before the face of God?' And I invoked my inextinguishable Name!"

2. Fragment B (apud Origen, Philocalia 23.15; preserved also in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica 6.11.64; Latin translation in Procopius of Gaza, Commentarius in Genesin 29):

  • Jacob reads the heavenly tablets / cosmic books of heaven, unrolling the future events, trials, and destinies of humankind.

3. Fragment C (apud Origen, Philocalia 23.19):

  • Origen summarizes and paraphrases Fragment A, confirming the angelic status of Jacob and citing Fragment B on the heavenly tablets.

Theological Significance: The Prayer of Joseph is of foundational importance for the history of ancient religions, witnessing to an extraordinary Jewish angelomorphic theology wherein a human patriarch is revealed as the primordial, pre-existent chief archangel of God (prōtogonos / "firstborn of all creation"). This concept provides an indispensable historical bridge between early Jewish wisdom/angelology and the Christological hymns of the New Testament (Col 1:15–18; Phil 2:6–11; John 1:1–18; Heb 1:5–6).
