2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Odes of Solomon (Syriac: Zmīrāthā d-Shlēmūn; Greek: Ὠιδαὶ Σολομῶντος / Ōidai Solomōntos; Latin: Odae Salomonis) is a collection of forty-two ecstatic lyrical hymns (Odes 1–42; Ode 2 is lost due to a physical lacuna in the manuscript traditions). Composed around the late 1st or early 2nd century CE (ca. 100–125 CE) in Roman Syria (most likely Antioch or Edessa/Osrhoene), the Odes constitute the earliest surviving Christian hymnal. Imbued with profound mystical intimacy, realized eschatology, and poetic passion, the collection reflects a bilingual Semitic-Hellenistic milieu that bridges the theological world of the Dead Sea Scrolls (Hodayot), the Gospel of John, and the letters of Saint Ignatius of Antioch.

Attributed pseudepigraphically to King Solomon—celebrated in 1 Kings 4:32 (LXX 3 Kgs 5:12) as having composed 1,005 songs—the Odes speak in the first-person voice of the redeemed believer, the prophet-singer, and at times Christ Himself (Christus loquens).

Major Thematic and Theological Motifs

1. The Crown / Wreath of Truth (Odes 1, 5, 9, 17):

  • The collection opens with the iconic image: "The Lord is on my head like a crown, and I shall not be without Him. Woven for me is the crown of truth, and it caused your branches to bud in me" (Ode 1:1–2). The crown represents divine life, truth, and imperishability.

2. The Living Water and the River of Life (Odes 6, 11, 30):

  • A river of the Holy Spirit flows from the divine throne, carrying living water across the whole earth: "A stream went forth and became a river great and broad... it carried away everything and broke down the Temple... all the thirsty upon earth drank, and thirst was relieved and quenched" (Ode 6:8–18).

3. The Circumcision of the Heart and the Robe of Light (Ode 11):

  • Preserved in Greek (Papyrus Bodmer XI) and Syriac: The singer's heart is circumcised by the Spirit; the old earthly garment is stripped away, and the soul is clothed in celestial light, translated into Paradise to feast on the fruits of the Tree of Life.

4. Startling Trinitarian and Maternal Imagery (Ode 19):

  • One of the most famous and unique passages in early Christian poetry: "A cup of milk was offered to me, and I drank it in the sweetness of the Lord's delight. The Son is the cup, and the Father is He who was milked; and the Holy Spirit is She who milked Him... The Holy Spirit opened her womb and gave birth to the Son without pain... without requiring a midwife" (19:1–9).

5. The Descent into Sheol / The Harrowing of Hell (Odes 17, 22, 42):

  • The triumphant voice of Christ recounting His descent into the underworld: "Sheol saw me and was shattered, and Death cast me up and many along with me. I was gall and vinegar to it, and I descended with it to the depth of its abyss... and I made a congregation of living men among its dead" (Ode 42:11–17).

6. The Gesture of Prayer as the Cross / Orante (Odes 27, 42):

  • The bodily posture of the orante—arms outstretched in prayer—is identified with the wood of the Cross: "I extended my hands and hallowed my Lord, for the expansion of my hands is His sign, and my outspreading is the upright cross" (Ode 27:1–3; 42:1–2).

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The Odes of Solomon are preserved across three ancient linguistic traditions:

A. The Syriac Manuscript Tradition

The principal and most complete text survives in two Syriac manuscripts where the Odes immediately precede the Psalms of Solomon:

1. MS H (Manchester, John Rylands University Library, Syriac MS 9, olim Cod. Harris 1):

  • Date: 16th century CE (paper codex).
  • Provenance: Discovered in 1909 by James Rendel Harris on the shelves of his study in Birmingham, having been brought from the Tigris region.
  • Preserves Odes 3:1b through 42:20 (Odes 1:1–3:1a lost due to missing leaves).

2. MS N (London, British Library, MS Add. 14538, fols. 148v–155v):

  • Date: 10th century CE (vellum codex in Estrangela script).
  • Provenance: St. Mary Deipara in the Nitrian Desert, Egypt (cataloged by W. Wright, 1872, Part 3, No. 936, pp. 1003–1008).
  • Preserves Odes 17:7 through 42:20.

B. The Greek Witness: Papyrus Bodmer XI

  • Papyrus Bodmer XI (G) (Cologny-Geneva, Bibliotheca Bodmeriana):
  • Date: 3rd century CE (early 4th c.).
  • Content: Preserves Ode 11 complete in Greek under the rubric Ὠιδὴ Σολομῶντος (Ōidē Solomōntos), bound within a famous miscellaneous papyrus codex alongside the Protoevangelium of James (Pap. Bodmer V) and 1–2 Peter.
  • Editio Princeps: Michel Testuz, Papyrus Bodmer XI (Cologny-Geneva, 1959).

C. The Coptic Gnostic Witness (Pistis Sophia)

  • London, British Library, MS Add. 5114 (The Askew Codex):
  • Date: 4th century CE (written in Sahidic Coptic).
  • Content: The gnostic treatise Pistis Sophia quotes five complete Odes in Sahidic Coptic, providing theological interpretations by the disciples:
  • Ode 1 (Pistis Sophia ch. 59)
  • Ode 5 (Pistis Sophia ch. 58)
  • Ode 6 (Pistis Sophia ch. 65)
  • Ode 22 (Pistis Sophia ch. 71)
  • Ode 25 (Pistis Sophia ch. 69)
  • Critical Edition: Carl Schmidt and Violet MacDermot, Pistis Sophia (Nag Hammadi Studies 9; Leiden: Brill, 1978).

D. Latin Patristic Witness

  • Lactantius (Divinae Institutiones IV.12.3, ca. 310 CE):
  • "Salomon in ode undevicesima ita dicit: Infirmatus est uterus virginis et accepit foetum et gravata est et facta est in multa miseratione mater virgo..."
  • This explicit citation of Ode 19:6 demonstrates that a Latin translation of the numbered Odes was circulating in North Africa by the early 4th century.
