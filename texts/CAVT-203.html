2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Syriac Apocryphal Psalms I–V comprise a corpus of five non-Masoretic poetic compositions traditionally appended to the canonical 150 Psalms in Eastern Syriac Psalter manuscripts (most notably Baghdad, Chaldean Patriarchate MS 1113 [formerly Diyarbakır 12t4] and BL Add. MS 14568).

Before the discovery of Cave 11 at Qumran in 1956, modern scholarship (beginning with William Wright in 1887, followed by Mingana, Harris, and Martin Noth in 1928–1930) knew these poems primarily as Christian or Jewish-Syriac curiosities preserved in the Peshitta textual stream. The field was revolutionized by J. A. Sanders's publication of the great Dead Sea Psalms Scroll (11QPsᵃ / 11Q5) in 1962–1965:

1. Psalm 151: Discovered in 11Q5 col. xxviii as two distinct Hebrew poetic compositions:

  • Psalm 151A (xxviii 3–12): A Davidic autobiographical hymn recountings his youth as a shepherd, his crafting of musical instruments (kinnor and nevel) to praise God, and his anointing by Samuel over his taller, more handsome brothers.
  • Psalm 151B (xxviii 13–14): A fragmentary hymn detailing David's combat with the Philistine giant Goliath and his decapitation with Goliath's own sword.
  • In the Greek Septuagint, these two separate Hebrew poems were redacted and condensed into a single Greek psalm (LXX Ps 151), designated idiógraphos eis Dabid éxōthen tou arithmoú ("an autograph of David outside the number"). The Syriac (ApocPs I), Old Latin, and Ge'ez (Ethiopic) versions derive from this Greek recension.

2. Psalm 154 (= Syriac Apocryphal Psalm II): Preserved in Hebrew in 11Q5 col. xviii 1–18 (with verses 1–2 reconstructed from Syriac and 4Q448). It is a sapiential-communal hymn praising God in the assembly of the righteous (qahal, ḥaverim), celebrating table fellowship, Torah study, and the glorification of YHWH over pagan sacrifices.

3. Psalm 155 (= Syriac Apocryphal Psalm III): Preserved in Hebrew in 11Q5 col. xxiv 3–18. A deeply personal penitential psalm and individual plea for deliverance from sin, judgment, and demonic oppression ("let not Satan rule over me, nor an unclean spirit"), structured closely upon the language of biblical psalms (especially Psalm 51).

4. Psalms 152 and 153 (= Syriac Apocryphal Psalms IV and V): These two poems are preserved uniquely in Syriac:

  • Psalm 152 (IV): "Spoken by David when he was contending with the lion and the wolf which took a sheep from his flock" (1 Sam 17:34–37). A prayer of distress invoking God's past salvation.
  • Psalm 153 (V): "Spoken by David when he gave thanks to God, who delivered him from the lion and the wolf and he slew both of them." A hymn of thanksgiving.
  • Linguistic and retroversion studies (Martin Noth, A. S. van der Woude, H. F. van Rooy) demonstrate that both compositions translate an original Hebrew Vorlage composed in late Second Temple Palestine (2nd–1st c. BCE), sharing the vocabulary, meter, and Davidic haggadah of the Qumran poetic scrolls.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

A. Hebrew Witnesses (Dead Sea Scrolls)

1. 11QPsᵃ (11Q5):

  • Date: Herodian script, ca. 30–50 CE.
  • Provenance: Qumran Cave 11; discovered 1956, editio princeps by J. A. Sanders (DJD IV, 1965).
  • Contents: 41 biblical psalms in non-traditional sequence, interspersed with seven non-Masoretic texts:
  • Col. xviii 1–18: Psalm 154 (vv. 3–19).
  • Col. xix 1–18: Plea for Deliverance (non-canonical penitential apotropaic psalm).
  • Col. xxi 11–17, xxii 1a: Sirach 51:13–30 (acrostic Hymn to Wisdom).
  • Col. xxii 1b–15: Apostrophe to Zion (alphabetic acrostic celebration of the New Jerusalem).
  • Col. xxiv 3–18: Psalm 155 (vv. 1–19).
  • Col. xxvi 9–15: Hymn to the Creator (celebrating cosmic order and the solar calendar).
  • Col. xxvii 2–11: David's Compositions (historical prose notice attributing 4,050 compositions to David: 3,600 tehillim and 450 shir for daily, Sabbath, and festival offerings).
  • Col. xxviii 3–12: Psalm 151A.
  • Col. xxviii 13–14: Psalm 151B.

2. 11QPsᵇ (11Q6):

  • Cave 11 fragments containing parts of Apostrophe to Zion (frr. 1–3) and Plea for Deliverance (frr. 4–5).

3. 4QPsᶠ (4Q88):

  • Cave 4 manuscript containing Apostrophe to Zion (cols. vii–viii), followed by two unique non-Masoretic psalms: Eschatological Hymn (col. ix) and Apostrophe to Judah (col. x).

4. 4Q448 (Prayer for King Jonathan and Psalm 154):

  • Column A preserves fragments corresponding to Hebrew Psalm 154:17–20, embedded before or alongside a liturgical prayer invoking blessing upon "King Jonathan" (Alexander Jannaeus, 103–76 BCE).

B. Syriac Witnesses

1. Baghdad, Library of the Chaldean Patriarchate, MS 1113 (formerly Diyarbakır, Archbishopric MS 12t4):

  • Date: 12th century (1113 CE).
  • Contents: Fols. 118b–120b contain all five Syriac Apocryphal Psalms (I–V), complete with detailed titles and marginal scholia describing the historical occasions in David's life.

2. London, British Library, Add. MS 14568:

  • Date: 6th century (one of the oldest surviving Syriac biblical manuscripts).
  • Contents: Fols. 49b–50a contain Syriac Apocryphal Psalms I, II (Ps 154), and III (Ps 155).

3. Manchester, John Rylands Library, Syr. MS 7:

  • Date: 16th century. Fol. 135a preserves Syriac Psalm 151 only.

4. Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, Sachau 209:

  • Witness for the liturgical reception of the apocryphal psalms.

C. Greek Witnesses (Psalm 151)

  • Preserved in all major uncial Bibles containing the Septuagint Psalter:
  • Codex Vaticanus (B, 03), 4th c.
  • Codex Sinaiticus (א, 01), 4th c.
  • Codex Alexandrinus (A, 02), 5th c. (titled Idiógraphos eis Dabid éxōthen tou arithmoú).
  • Zurich Purple Psalter (Turicensis, T), 7th c.

D. Latin and Ethiopic Witnesses

  • Old Latin (Vetus Latina): Preserved in Psalterium Veronense, Sangallense, Mozarabic psalter, and Roman liturgies (collated by A.-M. Denis and J. Verheyden).
  • Ge'ez (Mashafa Mazmur): Regular inclusion in standard Ethiopic Psalters following Psalm 150.
