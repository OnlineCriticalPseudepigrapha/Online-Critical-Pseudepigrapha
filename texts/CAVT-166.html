2. Overview & Textual Contents

The History of the Rechabites (also preserved under the titles The Story, Apocalypse, or Testament of Zosimus) is a composite ancient narrative recounting the supernatural voyage of the desert anchorite Zosimus to the pristine, earthly paradise of the Blessed Ones—identified as the descendants of Jonadab, son of Rechab (the Rechabites of Jeremiah 35).

Literary Stratigraphy: Frame and Jewish Core

Textual and source-critical investigations (notably by M. R. James, J. H. Charlesworth, E. G. Martin, and C. H. Knights) demonstrate that the work consists of two primary literary strata:

1. The Core "History of the Rechabites" (Chapters 8–10):

  • A distinct Second Temple Jewish haggadic narrative composed in Hebrew, Aramaic, or Jewish-Greek (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE).
  • Expanding on Jeremiah 35, the narrative explains how during the reign of King Zedekiah, the Rechabites were pressured to abandon the ascetic ordinances of their ancestor Jonadab (to drink no wine, sow no seed, and inhabit no stone houses).
  • When they refused, Zedekiah threw them into prison. God miraculously delivered them, and angels transported them across an impenetrable Ocean of Clouds and Darkness to an Edenic paradise isolated from the corrupt outside world.

2. The Christian Monastic Frame: "The Story of Zosimus" (Chapters 1–7, 11–18):

  • Composed in Byzantine monastic circles (4th–6th c. CE) to adapt the ancient Jewish utopian tale into a model of Christian ascetic contemplation (hesychia).
  • Zosimus spends 40 years praying in the desert to be granted a vision of the Blessed Ones. He is guided by an angel and animals to the banks of an impassable river of darkness/cloud (Eikos), conveyed across on the branches of two sentient trees, spends seven days interviewing the Rechabites, receives their written tablets, and is returned to his monastery to commit his testament to writing before dying.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

A. Greek Witnesses

  • A: Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, cod. gr. 1217, fols. 145a–153b (12th c.; base text for M. R. James's editio princeps).
  • B: Oxford, Bodleian Library, cod. Canon. gr. 19, fols. 289a–294a (15th/16th c.; preserves chs. 1.1–9.10).
  • C: Moscow, State Historical Museum, Synod. gr. 290 (Vladimir 14), fols. 112b–118b (16th c.).
  • D: Vatican, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, cod. Vat. gr. 1631, fols. 222b–227a (11th c.; missing fol. containing 16.8b–21.5a).
  • E: Moscow, Synod. gr. 3 (13th c.).
  • F: Moscow, Synod. gr. 351 (15th c.).
  • Lost Witnesses: Berlin, Deutsche Staatsbibliothek gr. qu. 22.7 (destroyed in WWII); Dresden, Bibliotheca Regia gr. A.187–18 (destroyed in WWII).

B. Syriac Witnesses

The Syriac textual tradition represents the most intact, coherent, and expansive recension of the History of the Rechabites:

  • Paris, BnF syr. 236, fols. 156b–167b (12th c.; published by F. Nau, 1898–1899).
  • Birmingham, Selly Oak Colleges, Mingana Syr. 21, fols. 17b–26b (ca. 1600 CE); and Mingana Syr. 40, fols. 26a–34a (ca. 1750 CE).
  • Cambridge, MA, Harvard College Library, Syr. 33 (formerly Union Theological Seminary 33).
  • Tehran, Private Chaldean Manuscript (dated 16th c., described by A. Desreumaux, 1994).
  • Krasnoyarsk / St. Petersburg MS (Syriac restored MS studied by J.-M. Sauget).

C. Ethiopic (Ge'ez) Witnesses

Known as The History of the Blessed Ones Who Lived in the Days of Jeremiah the Prophet (often included in the Ethiopic Acts of the Martyrs and the Synaxarium):

  • London, British Library, Orient. 660 and 661; Orient. 683, fols. 87a–98b (edited and translated by E. A. Wallis Budge, 1896/1928).
  • Paris, BnF, d'Abbadie MSS 145 and 179.

D. Slavonic, Arabic, and Armenian Versions

  • Old Church Slavonic: Widespread in early Russian and South Slavic miscellanies under the title The Journey of Zosimus to the Brahmans/Blessed (edited by I. Franko, A. I. Iatsimirskii, and N. S. Tikhonravov). The transliteration Raxmanom demonstrates direct lexical cross-pollination with the Alexander Romance traditions of the Indian Brahmans.
  • Arabic and Karshuni: Preserved in dozens of Christian-Arabic manuscripts throughout the Levant and Egypt (catalogued by Georg Graf and E. G. Martin).
  • Armenian: Preserved in synaxarion collections and monastic anthologies.
