2. Overview & Textual Contents

Joseph and Aseneth (CAVT 105) is a Greek narrative expanding Gen 41:45, where Pharaoh gives Joseph Aseneth, daughter of Potiphera priest of On, as his wife. Chapters 1–21 tell of Aseneth's rejection of her idols, her repentance, and her transformation through the visit of a heavenly man, followed by her marriage to Joseph. Chapters 22–29 narrate a plot by Pharaoh's son, aided by Dan and Gad, to abduct Aseneth, which is foiled by Benjamin, Levi and Simeon.

3. Date, Language & Provenance

Most scholars place the work in Egyptian Judaism between the 1st c. BCE and the early 2nd c. CE, though a later date and Christian authorship have also been argued (R.S. Kraemer, When Aseneth Met Joseph, 1998). It survives in Greek in two main textual forms, a longer and a shorter, and in Syriac, Armenian, Latin, Serbian Slavonic, Modern Greek and Romanian versions.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The work was translated into six ancient languages and enjoyed enormous popularity throughout the Byzantine, Syriac, and medieval Latin worlds:

A. Greek Tradition

Preserved in sixteen manuscripts classified into four distinct recensional families ($a, b, c, d$):

  • Family $a$ (Longer Recension): Best preserves the original semitizing Greek narrative style:
  • MS A (Vatican, BAV, Cod. Vat. gr. 364, fols. 223r–243v): 11th/12th century; base text of Batiffol's editio princeps.
  • MS C (Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Barocci 148, fols. 75v–92v): 15th-century manuscript containing five famous full-page color miniatures illustrating the narrative.
  • MS O (Sinai, Saint Catherine’s Monastery, MS gr. 504): 10th century; the oldest extant Greek witness.
  • MS P (Mount Athos, Konstamonitou Monastery 14): 15th century.
  • MS Q (Vatican, BAV, Cod. Vat. Palatinus gr. 364): 15th century.
  • MS R (Sinai, Saint Catherine’s Monastery, MS gr. 530): 15th/16th century.
  • Family $b$ (Shorter Recension): Smoothed, classicizing text chosen by Marc Philonenko:
  • MS E (Mount Athos, Vatopedi Monastery 600): 15th century.
  • MS F (Bucharest, Romanian Academy MS gr. 966): 17th century.
  • MS G (Greeley Collection, MS McKell): ca. 1580 CE; illustrated.
  • MS W (Sinai, Saint Catherine’s Monastery, MS gr. 1976): 17th century.
  • Family $c$: Late text represented in Jerusalem Patriarchate MSS (Panhagios Taphos 73, Saba 1802).
  • Family $d$: Mixed branch: MS B (Vat. Pal. gr. 17; 11th cent.) and MS D (Bodleian Barocci 147; 15th cent.).

B. Syriac Tradition

  • Preserved in the 6th-century historical chronicle of Pseudo-Zacharias Rhetor (Historia Ecclesiastica 1.6; London, BL MS Add. 17202, copied ca. 569 CE).
  • Translated from Greek into Syriac in 559 CE by Moses of Ingila; provides the earliest fixed historical anchor for the Greek text (ed. J. P. N. Land 1870; E. W. Brooks 1919).

C. Latin Tradition

  • Recension L$^1$: Translated from a Greek Family $a$ manuscript in the late 12th century; preserved in nine manuscripts (e.g., Cambridge, Corpus Christi College 288). Incorporated and epitomized by Vincent of Beauvais in his monumental encyclopedia Speculum Historiale (ca. 1250 CE, Book 1, chs. 118–124).
  • Recension L$^2$: Independent 15th-century Renaissance humanist translation.

D. Armenian Tradition

  • Preserved in 45 manuscripts classified into six groups, translated from an early Greek recension between the 5th and 7th centuries CE (ed. Yovsēp'ianc' 1896; Christoph Burchard).

E. Slavonic, Ethiopic, Romanian, and Middle English Versions

  • Preserved in medieval Old Church Slavonic manuscripts (ed. I. Franko; S. Novaković); Ethiopic Ge'ez florilegia; and an early Middle English poetic paraphrase.
