2. Overview & Textual Contents

3 Baruch (the Greek Apocalypse of Baruch) describes Baruch, grieving over the destruction of Jerusalem, being led by an angel through five heavens. There he sees the punishment of the tower-builders, the dragon of Hades, the sun and moon, and Michael receiving the prayers of the righteous.

3. Date, Language & Provenance

It survives in Greek and Slavonic. A Jewish work of the 1st–2nd c. CE with Christian revision is the common view, though some regard it as Christian.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

A. The Greek Tradition

  • London MS Add. 34060: London, British Library, MS Add. 34060 (fols. 560v–565r; 15th c.) — discovered and edited by Montague Rhodes James (Apocrypha Anecdota II, 1897).
  • Andros MS 46: Andros (Greece), Monastery of the Hagia (Panachrantos), MS 46 (fols. 195r–202v; 16th c.) — collated and published by Jean-Claude Picard (PVTG 2, 1967).
  • Note on Integrity: Both Greek manuscripts conclude abruptly in chapter 17, likely omitting an original ascension to a sixth or seventh heaven.

B. The Slavonic Tradition

  • Transmitted widely across the South Slavic and East Slavic worlds, preserved in over a dozen manuscripts dating from the 13th to 17th centuries, divided into two distinct recensions:
  • Short Recension: Emphasizes dualistic and folkloric elements; preserved in the 15th-century Petrisov Zbornik (MS Z; 1468 CE) and Belgrade National Library MS 651 (MS N; 13th c.).
  • Long Recension: Represents a fuller liturgical and narrative text; preserved in Belgrade MS 321 (MS S; 14th c.) and Moscow State Historical Museum Synod. 211.
  • Edited and classified critically by H. E. Gaylord (1983/1987).

  ** The standard edition. Presents the Greek text based on British Museum ms Add. 10073 and Andros (A), Monastery of the Hagia ms 46 (B). Picard explains that these two mss are extraordinarily close in their readings: "B provides no variant of significance from A, and often both provide the same errors, of both grammar and orthography" (p. 68). There are only two instances in which material is omitted by B but not by A: one in the title and the other in 6.16. Hence Picard's text remains essentially a transcription of ms A. While Picard has introduced many emmendations to the text, these are primarily to correct the orthography or misreadings resulting from phonological confusion (especially of vowels).
