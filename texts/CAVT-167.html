2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Apocalypse of Elijah is an early Jewish-Christian / Christian prophetic apocalypse composed likely in Egypt in the late 3rd century CE (incorporating earlier Jewish and eschatological traditions). It survives predominantly in Coptic (Akhmimic and Sahidic dialects), with an important Greek papyrus witness and patristic citations.

A. Greek Tradition

1. P. Med. Laurenz. PSI 7 (Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Papiri della Società Italiana 7):

  • 4th-century CE papyrus leaf preserving Greek text parallel to Akhmimic ch. 5:30–32 (the eschatological battle and cosmic judgment).
  • Editions:
  • Aland, Kurt. Repertorium der griechischen christlichen Papyri. I. Biblische Papyri. PTS 18. Berlin/New York: Walter de Gruyter, 1976, p. 368.
  • Denis, Albert-Marie. Fragmenta pseudepigraphorum quae supersunt graeca. PVTG 3. Leiden: Brill, 1970, pp. 103–104.
  • Denis, Albert-Marie. Concordance grecque des pseudépigraphes d'Ancien Testament. Louvain-la-Neuve: Université Catholique de Louvain, 1987, p. 904.
  • Pietersma, Albert, Stephen T. Comstock, and Harold W. Attridge. "Appendix: The Greek Apocalypse of Elijah." In The Apocalypse of Elijah Based on Papyrus Chester Beatty 2018. SBLTT 19, PS 9. Chico, CA: Scholars Press, 1981, pp. 90–94.

2. Patristic Quotations & External Testimonies:

  • Origen (Commentarius in Matthaeum 27.9 [GCS 38, p. 250]): explicitly identifies 1 Cor 2:9 ("Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard...") as deriving from in Secretis Eliae (Ἀπόκρυφον Ἠλίου).
  • Didymus the Blind (Commentarius in Ecclesiasten, P. Tura): preserves citations analyzed by D. Lührmann (ZAW 104 [1992]: 231–249 at 245–249).
  • Jerome (Epistula 57.9 ad Pammachium; In Isaiam 64.4): debates whether 1 Cor 2:9 is derived from Isaiah 64:4 or the apocryphal Ascensio Isaiae / Apocalypsis Eliae.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 42.12.3): references apocryphal Elijah traditions.
  • Stichometry of Nicephorus (stichoi 316) and the Athanasian Synopsis (identifying an apocryphal book of Elijah).

B. Coptic Manuscript Tradition

The work is preserved across two main Egyptian dialects:

1. Akhmimic Witnesses:

  • Berlin, Staatliche Museen, Ägyptisches Museum P. 1862: Late 3rd or early 4th century CE. Contains substantial continuous portions of the apocalypse.
  • Paris, Bibliothèque Nationale de France, MS copte 135, fols. 1–7: 3rd/4th century CE. First published by Urbain Bouriant (1884).

2. Sahidic Witnesses:

  • Dublin, Chester Beatty Library, Papyrus Chester Beatty 2018: 4th/5th century CE papyrus codex; acquired in the mid-20th century. Provides the most complete continuous Sahidic text of chapters 1:1–3:28 and 4:18–5:39.
  • Paris, Bibliothèque Nationale de France, MS copte 135, fols. 8–14: 3rd/4th century CE; preserves Sahidic fragments parallel to the Akhmimic text.
  • London, British Library, MS Or. 7594: 4th century CE papyrus codex preserving the title/colophon ⲧⲁⲡⲟⲕⲁⲗⲩⲯⲓⲥ ⲛ̄ⲏⲗⲓⲁⲥ (published by E.A.W. Budge, 1912).
