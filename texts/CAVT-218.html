2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Ascension of Isaiah (Ascensio Isaiae / Ἀναβατικὸν Ἡσαΐου) is a composite apocalypse combining ancient Jewish haggadic traditions with early Jewish-Christian visionary literature. Redacted in its final form in the late 2nd or early 3rd century CE (likely in Roman Syria or Asia Minor), the work consists of three historically and thematically distinct components:

1. The Martyrdom of Isaiah (Asc. Isa. 1:1–3:12; 5:1–16):

  • An ancient Jewish narrative composed in Semitic (Hebrew or Aramaic) during the 1st century BCE or 1st century CE. King Hezekiah instructs his wicked son Manasseh. Upon Manasseh’s accession, the demonic ruler Beliar (Sammael) inspires apostasy and idolatry in Jerusalem. Isaiah flees with his prophetic disciples (including Habakkuk and Joel) to a mountain near Bethlehem. The Samaritan false prophet Belkira (Malkira) denounces Isaiah to Manasseh. Isaiah is arrested and horribly executed by being sawn in two with a wooden saw (serratus sectus est). Throughout the ordeal, Isaiah converses with the Holy Spirit, shedding neither tears nor uttering cries.
  • Ancient External Attestations: Alluded to in the New Testament (Heb 11:37: "they were sawn in two"); Justin Martyr (Dialogus cum Tryphone 120.5); Tertullian (De patientia 14; Scorpiace 8); Origen (Comm. in Mt. 13.57; Epistula ad Africanum 9); and the Babylonian Talmud (b. Yevamot 49b; b. Sanhedrin 103b).

2. The Testament of Hezekiah (Asc. Isa. 3:13–4:22):

  • An independent Christian apocalyptic interpolation dating from the late 1st century CE (ca. 80–100 CE). Isaiah delivers a prospective vision of the descent of the Beloved (Christ), His ministry with the Twelve, His crucifixion, and His resurrection. He foretells the corruption, lawlessness, and greed of the post-apostolic elders and bishops, followed by the descent of the cosmic prince Beliar in the likeness of the matricidal Roman emperor Nero ("a lawless king, the murderer of his mother"). Beliar-Nero works deceptive signs, establishes worldwide imperial worship, and persecutes the faithful for 3 years, 7 months, and 27 days (1335 days), after which the Lord arrives from the seventh heaven with his angelic host to cast Beliar and his armies into Gehenna.

3. The Vision / Ascension of Isaiah (Asc. Isa. 6:1–11:43):

  • An early 2nd-century CE (ca. 110–130 CE) visionary apocalypse originally circulating independently. In the 20th year of Hezekiah, Isaiah falls into an ecstatic trance before the king and prophets. A radiant angel guides him in an ascent through the seven heavens: in the second through fifth heavens, he witnesses the angelic orders praising the divine throne; in the sixth heaven, all praise is directed upward in unison; in the seventh heaven, Isaiah sees the righteous souls clothed in light, with their garments, thrones, and crowns awaiting them.
  • The Cosmic Descensus and Ascensus: God commissions the Beloved to descend through all seven heavens down to the earthly realm and the underworld (Sheol). In His descent, the Beloved conceals His glory, transforming His appearance in each heaven so that the resident archons do not recognize Him. On earth, He is born miraculously of the virgin Mary without labour or pain (11:2–14), lives unrecognized, is crucified by the rulers of the cosmos under the instigation of Beliar, rises on the third day, and ascends back to the seventh heaven in full, dazzling majesty, receiving the adoration of all cosmic powers.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

While fragmentary remains survive in Greek, Latin, Coptic, and Slavonic, the complete text of chapters 1–11 is preserved only in classical Ge'ez (Ethiopic):

A. Ethiopic (Ge'ez) Tradition

The primary witness for the entire work, preserved in nine manuscripts:

  • MS A (Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Aeth. d. 13, fols. 95r–115r): 15th-century codex; the base text for Laurence and Dillmann.
  • MS B (London, British Library, MS Or. 501, fols. 62r–69v): 15th century.
  • MS C (London, British Library, MS Or. 503, fols. 57r–62v): 18th century.
  • MS E (EMML 1768, fols. 83r–88v): 15th century.
  • MS F (EMML 3067, fols. 99r–117v): 15th century.
  • MS G (Vatican, BAV, Cod. Vat. Aeth. 263, fols. 85v–104v): 14th/15th century.
  • MS H (Paris, BnF, d'Abbadie 195, fols. 74r–87r): 17th/18th century.
  • MS I (Paris, BnF, d'Abbadie 156, fols. 46r–52v): 18th century.
  • MS L (Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cod. Aeth. 63): 18th/19th century.

B. Greek Tradition

1. Amherst Papyrus 1 (P. Amherst I.1; London, Pierpont Morgan Library / British Library):

  • 5th-century CE papyrus preserving Greek Asc. Isa. 2:4b–4:4a (the flight of Isaiah and the beginning of the Testament of Hezekiah).
  • Editio princeps: Grenfell, B. P., and A. S. Hunt. The Amherst Papyri. London, 1900, 1:1–22.

2. Patristic Citations:

  • Origen (Comm. in Mt. 13.57; Ep. ad Afr. 9).
  • Didymus the Blind (Comm. in Eccl., P. Tura; ed. D. Lührmann, ZAW 104 [1992]: 231–249 at 233–239).
  • Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 40.2.2–4; 67.3.3).

3. Medieval Greek "Legend of Isaiah":

  • A Byzantine hagiographical adaptation of the Ascension preserved in Paris BnF gr. 1534 (12th cent.) and Vat. Palat. gr. 27 (10th/11th cent.); edited by C. Tischendorf (1866) and B. Flusin in CCSA 8 (1995).

C. Latin Tradition

Two independent partial Latin witnesses:

  • Lat$^1$ (Vatican, BAV, Cod. Vat. Lat. 5750, fols. 278–284): 5th/6th-century palimpsest uncial preserving Asc. Isa. 2:14–3:13 and 7:1–19 (ed. Angelo Mai 1828; C. Leonardi in CCSA 7, 1995).
  • Lat$^2$ (Visio mirabilis Ysaiae Prophetae): 12th-century Latin translation of chapters 6–11 published by Antonius de Fantis in Venice (1517), reprinted by R. H. Charles (1900) and C. Leonardi in CCSA 7 (1995).

D. Coptic Tradition

  • Akhmimic Fragments: Louvain University Library Coptic MS 1 (5th cent., destroyed in WWII); preserved Asc. Isa. 9:35–11:23 (ed. L. T. Lefort, Muséon 1938, 1939).
  • Sahidic Fragments: Paris BnF copte 135 and Cairo Coptic Museum fragments (ed. Pierre Lacau, Muséon 1946; Paolo Bettiolo in CCSA 7, 1995).

E. Slavonic Tradition

  • Preserved in over 20 medieval South Slavic and East Slavic manuscripts (e.g., the 12th-century Uspenskij Sbornik); contains an archaic translation of the Vision of Isaiah (chs. 6–11) translated from an early Greek recension (ed. A. Popov 1879; Alda Giambelluca Kossova in CCSA 7, 1995).
