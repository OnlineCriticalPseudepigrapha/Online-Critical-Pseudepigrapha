2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Psalms of Solomon (PssSol) is a Jewish pseudepigraphical collection of eighteen poetic psalms composed in Judea during the turbulent midpoint of the first century BCE (ca. 63–37 BCE) and brought into its final composite form prior to the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE. Pseudepigraphically attributed to King Solomon—celebrated in biblical tradition for his wisdom, proverbs, and thousands of songs (cf. 1 Kgs 4:32; canonical Ps 72)—the collection captures the acute communal trauma, factional conflict, and fervent eschatological longings of Jewish society following the collapse of Hasmonean sovereignty and the Roman conquest of Jerusalem by Pompey the Great in 63 BCE.

Although not preserved in the canonical Hebrew Bible or among the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Psalms of Solomon was faithfully transmitted through Christian ecclesiastical channels in the Greek East. Ancient canonical catalogues—most notably the fifth-century table of contents (Pinax) of Codex Alexandrinus (which lists the eighteen psalms among the antilegomena following 1–2 Clement), the sixth-century Synopsis Scripturae Sacrae (pseudo-Athanasius), and the ninth-century Stichometry of Nicephorus (which measures the work at 2,100 stichoi)—demonstrate that the collection was read and copied as a significant ecclesiastical appendix to Septuagint and New Testament codices.

Spanning an array of classical biblical poetic genres, the collection includes communal laments (PssSol 1, 2, 8), individual prayers of thanksgiving and penitence (PssSol 3, 5, 6, 9, 13, 15, 16), wisdom and didactic reflections (PssSol 4, 10, 14), and royal messianic hymns (PssSol 17, 18). It stands alongside the Dead Sea Scrolls as one of the most vital textual witnesses to late Second Temple Palestinian Jewish theology, articulating a profound theodicy in the face of foreign subjugation, an acute ethical dualism between the "righteous" and "sinners," an explicit hope in the bodily resurrection of the righteous to eternal life, and the most detailed pre-Christian portrait of a personal, Davidic Messiah (huios Dauid) in ancient Jewish literature.

3. Date, Language & Provenance

Date and Historical Setting

The historical backdrop of the Psalms of Solomon is anchored in the dramatic political convulsions of first-century BCE Judea. Three psalms in particular—2, 8, and 17—contain vivid, transparent historical allusions that allow modern scholars to date their composition with remarkable precision:

  • The Siege and Fall of Jerusalem (63 BCE): Psalm 8 recounts the approach of a foreign conqueror from the ends of the earth who was welcomed by the city's corrupt leadership, assaulted the fortified walls with battering rams (8:15–21; cf. 2:1), and desecrated the Temple sanctuary, trampling the holy altar with pagan feet (2:2). As first recognized by F. K. Movers in the nineteenth century, this corresponds exactly to the Roman siege led by Pompey the Great in 63 BCE, intervening in the civil war between the Hasmonean brothers Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II as recounted by Flavius Josephus (Ant. 14.3–4; J.W. 1.6–7).
  • The Denunciation of Late Hasmonean Usurpation: The psalms deliver a scathing theological polemic against the native Hasmonean priest-kings (Aristobulus II, Hyrcanus II, and Alexander Jannaeus). The psalmist charges them with unlawfully arrogating royal majesty to themselves without Davidic covenantal legitimacy (17:5–8) and defiling the altar, sanctuary, and sacrificial rites with ritual impurity and incest (8:11–13, 22; 2:3–14). The Roman assault is thus interpreted not as a tragic defeat of God's people, but as a deserved divine punishment upon an apostate aristocracy.
  • The Ignominious Fate of Pompey (48 BCE): Psalm 2:26–27 provides a decisive terminus post quem for the collection, describing the dishonorable end of the Roman conqueror: "I did not have to wait long before God showed me the insolent one lying dead on the mountains of Egypt, despised more than the smallest on land and sea, his body decomposed by the waves with no one to bury him." This matches Pompey's assassination on the beach of Pelusium in Egypt in 48 BCE following his defeat at the Battle of Pharsalus, where his headless body was left exposed on the shore.
  • Herod the Great's Siege and the Atkinson Redating Thesis: While earlier scholars (e.g., Ewald, Bretschneider, Cellier) proposed obsolete dates ranging from Ptolemy I to Titus, Kenneth Atkinson ("Toward a Redating of the Psalms of Solomon," 1998; "Herod the Great, Sosius, and the Siege of Jerusalem," 1996) argues that Psalm 17 reflects the bloody siege of Jerusalem in 37/36 BCE by Herod the Great and the Roman general Gaius Sosius. Atkinson suggests that the collection spans the historical transition from the late Hasmonean period to the early Herodian era (ca. 63–30 BCE), reflecting an ongoing community that weathered both crises.

The complete absence of any allusion to the cataclysmic destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE firmly establishes that the collection attained its final redacted form before 70 CE, with the majority of psalms composed between 63 and 40 BCE (as proposed by R. B. Wright, E. P. Sanders, and M. de Jonge).

Authorship, Provenance, and Sectarian Milieu

The collection was composed in Judea, almost certainly in Jerusalem or its immediate environs. The identity of the community behind the psalms has been one of the most vigorously debated problems in Second Temple scholarship:

  • The Pharisaic Hypothesis: Pioneered by Julius Wellhausen (Die Pharisäer und die Sadducäer, 1874) and popularized by H. E. Ryle and M. R. James in their landmark edition Psalms of the Pharisees, Commonly Called the Psalms of Solomon (1891), this view held that the authors were Pharisees opposing the Sadducean Hasmonean hierarchy. The thesis was supported by the poems' fierce devotion to Torah, belief in divine sovereignty alongside human free will, insistence on bodily resurrection, and strict purity regulations. Mikael Winninge (Sinners and the Righteous, 1995) has defended a modified version of this stance.
  • The Post-Qumran Critique: The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls decisively shattered the exclusivity of the Pharisaic identification. As demonstrated by J. O'Dell (1961) and R. B. Wright (1972), doctrines such as divine retribution, bodily resurrection, messianic deliverance, and hatred for Hasmonean corruption were not distinctive to Pharisees, but were widely shared across the broader apocalyptic and pietistic streams of Palestinian Judaism. The internal evidence proves firmly that the authors were non-Sadducean, but does not identify them uniquely as Pharisees.
  • The Essene and Qumran-Adjacent Hypotheses: André Dupont-Sommer (1973) proposed that the psalms originated among the Essenes, a hypothesis further refined by P. N. Franklyn (1987), who posited a previously unrecorded branch of the Essene movement. However, no copies or fragments of the Psalms of Solomon have been found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the references to fleeing into the wilderness (PsSol 17:16) reflect temporary flight during siege warfare rather than permanent communal withdrawal to Qumran.
  • The Zadokite Displacement Model: Advanced by Heerak Christian Kim (Zadokite Propaganda in the Late Second Temple Period, 2014), this theory links the collection with displaced Oniad/Zadokite priests who were ousted from the Temple cult by the Hasmoneans and who produced polemical psalms protesting their disenfranchisement and calling for cultic restoration.
  • The Modern Consensus: Unknown Jerusalem Pietists: The dominant contemporary perspective, articulated by Kenneth Atkinson (I Cried to the Lord, 2004) and endorsed by E. P. Sanders, concludes that the authors cannot be identified with any known historical sect (Pharisees, Sadducees, or Essenes). Rather, the psalms were composed by an unknown, pietistic Jerusalem circle—self-designated as "the assemblies of the devout" (συναγωγαὶ ὁσίων, PsSol 17:16)—rooted in traditional Hasidic piety (Büchler 1922; Flusser 1984; Hann 1988) and maintaining strict covenantal fidelity amid socio-political marginalization.

Original Language and Translation Character

The overwhelming majority of modern scholars (including Ryle and James, G. B. Gray, Emil Schürer, Joseph L. Trafton, and Robert B. Wright) maintain that the Psalms of Solomon was originally composed in Hebrew, and subsequently translated into Greek, from which the Syriac version later derived (or, as Trafton and Grant Ward argue, with both versions reflecting independent access to a common Hebrew Vorlage). The case for a lost Hebrew original rests on several converging lines of philological evidence:

  • Pervasive Semitic Syntax: The Greek text exhibits continuous coordinate clauses linked by καί, construct-state periphrases, rigid Hebrew word order, and idioms that represent word-for-word calques of classical biblical Hebrew poetry.
  • Demonstrable Translation Errors: Several obscure or impossible Greek readings are explicable only as misreadings or graphic confusion of Hebrew consonants. For example, at PsSol 2:25, the translator appears to have misread Hebrew rymhl ("to change/alter") as rm'l ("to say"), producing the ungrammatical Greek phrase τοῦ εἰπεῖν. Similarly, at PsSol 2:26, the description of Pompey's insolence (ἡ ὑπερηφανία αὐτοῦ) likely reflects a graphic confusion of Hebrew g'wtw ("his pride") for gwytw ("his corpse").
  • Intertextual Dependence with Baruch: The undeniable literary relationship between Psalm of Solomon 11 and Baruch 5:5–8 demonstrates that one work drew upon the other. Because PsSol 11 displays superior poetic unity and structural cohesion, scholars widely judge that Baruch translated or adapted a pre-existing Hebrew Vorlage of Psalm 11.
  • The Syriac Evidence: Comprehensive philological analysis by Joseph L. Trafton (1985, 1986) indicates that while the Syriac text shows close affinity with the Greek manuscript family of Group 253, several Syriac readings preserve genuine Semitic idioms or variants that suggest access to a Semitic Vorlage.

A vocal minority (beginning with Hilgenfeld in 1871 and revisited by some contemporary linguists) has argued for direct Greek composition in intentional imitation of the Septuagint. However, as Kenneth Atkinson notes in the NETS commentary, the persistent "lexical stereotyping" (e.g., translating Hebrew hasid uniformly as ὅσιος and hesed as ἔλεος) and syntactical stiffness are standard hallmarks of translation Greek rather than Hellenistic Greek composition.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The Greek Manuscript Tradition

The Greek text of the Psalms of Solomon survives in eleven manuscripts dating from the tenth to the sixteenth century CE. Through the stemmatic analyses of Robert R. Hann (The Manuscript History of the Psalms of Solomon, 1982), Robert B. Wright (The Psalms of Solomon: A Critical Edition of the Greek Text, 2007), and Felix Albrecht (Göttingen Septuagint XII,3, 2018), these witnesses are classified into four distinct textual families, alongside ancient indirect witnesses:

Ancient Indirect Attestation

  • Codex Alexandrinus (MS A, 5th century CE): Listed in the table of contents (Pinax) under disputed books (antilegomena) immediately following 1–2 Clement with the entry ΨΑΛΜΟΙ ΣΟΛΟΜΩΝΤΟΣ ΙΗ ("Eighteen Psalms of Solomon"). The folios containing the text at the very end of the great codex were lost prior to the modern era, but the entry proves its canonical reception in late antique Egypt.
  • Patristic Stichometries: Cited in the pseudo-Athanasian Synopsis Scripturae Sacrae (early 6th c.) and the Stichometry of Nicephorus (Patriarch of Constantinople, 9th c.), which reckons the work at 2,100 stichoi among the ecclesiastical books.

The Syriac Tradition

The Psalms of Solomon is also preserved in the Syriac tradition across five manuscripts (critical edition by W. Baars, 1972):

  • MS S (7th century CE): A fragmentary marginal text preserved in the margin of a manuscript of the Hymns of Severus of Antioch (British Library, Add. 14538).
  • The Complete Syriac Codices: Manchester, John Rylands Library, Syr. 9 (the celebrated 16th-century "Harris codex"); Birmingham, Selly Oak Colleges, Mingana Syr. 163; Cambridge, University Library, Add. 2012; and Jerusalem, St. Mark's Monastery, Syr. 161.

In the Syriac manuscripts, the eighteen psalms are appended directly to the Odes of Solomon, numbered continuously as Odes 43–60. Philological evaluations by Joseph L. Trafton (1985) demonstrate that the Syriac translation differs from the Greek in only about 62 verses (roughly 10%) and shows an intimate affinity with the Greek archetype of Group 253, while in several passages reflecting independent Semitic idioms.

History of Printed Editions

  • Editio Princeps (1626): First printed by the Jesuit scholar Juan Luis de la Cerda in his Adversaria Sacra (Lyon, 1626), based on a transcript of MS 149 rediscovered in Augsburg by David Hoeschel (1556–1617).
  • Oscar von Gebhardt (1895): Die Psalmen Salomo’s; the foundation of modern stemmatic criticism, expanding the known Greek witnesses from five to eight manuscripts through Mount Athos discoveries.
  • Alfred Rahlfs (1935): Septuaginta; incorporated eight manuscripts, providing the widely adopted standard chapter and verse numbering.
  • Robert B. Wright (2007): The Psalms of Solomon: A Critical Edition of the Greek Text; the first complete edition to collate all eleven Greek manuscripts and four Syriac codices with high-resolution digital imaging.
  • Felix Albrecht (2018): Psalmi Salomonis (Göttingen Septuagint XII,3); the definitive monumental edition providing exhaustive stemmatic apparatus, patristic citations, and orthographic documentation.
