2. Overview & Textual Contents

4 Ezra (chapters 3–14 of the composite book preserved in the Latin Vulgate as 2 Esdras / 4 Esdras) is a Jewish apocalypse composed in Roman Palestine (Judea) around 95–100 CE, thirty years after the Roman destruction of the Second Temple by Titus in 70 CE (historicized under the pseudonym of "Salathiel who is also Ezra" thirty years after the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem, 3:1). Written originally in Hebrew or Aramaic, translated early into Greek, and transmitted through multiple ancient versions, the apocalypse is structured into seven visionary episodes:

1. First Episode (3:1–5:20): Ezra laments in Babylon over the desolation of Zion and the prosperity of pagan Rome. The archangel Uriel rebukes him, declaring that human understanding cannot fathom divine ways, that the present age is pregnant with evil because of Adam's "evil heart" (cor malignum), and that the end-time harvest must await the predetermined number of the righteous.

2. Second Episode (5:21–6:34): Ezra questions why God delivered His chosen people to the nations. Uriel reveals that creation has grown old like an aging mother, and explains the transition of cosmic epochs: "Esau is the end of this age, and Jacob is the beginning of the age that follows" (6:9).

3. Third Episode (6:35–9:25): The lengthiest theological dialogue, exploring the fate of souls after death, the intermediate rest in celestial chambers/promptuaries, the 400-year temporal kingdom of the Messiah, the final death of all mortals (including the Messiah) followed by seven days of primeval silence, the universal resurrection, and the irreversible judgment where no intercession is possible for the damned (the famous "Lost Fragment," 7:36–105).

4. Fourth Episode (9:26–10:59): Ezra sees a weeping, disheveled woman mourning the sudden death of her only son on his wedding night. As Ezra consoles her, she is transfigured into a vast, shining, founded city. Uriel explains that the woman is Zion, her thirty barren years represent the period before Solomon, her son is the Temple destroyed by the Romans, and the radiant city is the eschatological heavenly Jerusalem.

5. Fifth Episode: The Eagle Vision (11:1–12:51): An imperial eagle with twelve wings, three heads, and eight contrary winglets arises from the sea, exercising oppressive global dominion. A roaring lion emerges from the forest and pronounces the eagle's annihilation. Uriel identifies the eagle as the fourth kingdom of Daniel 7 (Rome, reigning from Julius Caesar and Augustus through the Flavian emperors Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian), and the lion as the Davidic Messiah who will judge and destroy the empire.

6. Sixth Episode: The Man from the Sea (13:1–58): A cosmic man rises from the depths of the sea, flies with the clouds of heaven, carves out a great mountain (Mount Zion) without hands, incinerates a hostile attacking army with a stream of fire and flaming breath from his mouth, and then gathers a peaceful multitude: the lost Ten Tribes of Israel, who had crossed the Euphrates to the distant, unpolluted land of Arzareth.

7. Seventh Episode: The Voice from the Bush and the Restoration of the Scriptures (14:1–48): God calls Ezra from a thornbush as He did Moses. Ezra drinks a cup of glowing water like fire, receiving divine wisdom for forty days. Assisted by five rapid scribes, he dictates 94 books: 24 exoteric books to be made public for the worthy and unworthy alike (the Hebrew biblical canon), and 70 esoteric books reserved exclusively for the wise.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The original Semitic text is lost, but the text is preserved across eight major ancient language traditions, all translated directly or indirectly from a lost Greek translation:

A. Latin Tradition (Versio Latina)

The Latin version is the primary witness for Western biblical transmission. It falls into two groups depending on the presence of the "Missing Fragment" (7:36–105), which was omitted from the Clementine Vulgate because a single leaf had been deliberately cut out of the primary archetype:

  • MS S (Codex Sangermanensis I; Paris, BnF lat. 11504–11505): Copied at Saint-Germain-des-Prés in 821/822 CE. Archetype of all European Vulgate manuscripts lacking 7:36–105.
  • The Eight Independent Latin Manuscripts (preserving the missing section):
  • MS A (Codex Ambianensis; Amiens, Bibliothèque Municipale 10): 9th-century manuscript in which Robert L. Bensly discovered the "Lost Fragment" in 1874.
  • MS C (Codex Complutensis 1; Madrid, Universidad Central 31): 9th/10th century.
  • MS E (Codex Epternacensis; Luxembourg, Bibliothèque Nationale 264): Dated 1051–1081 CE.
  • MS L (Codex Legionensis 2; León, Real Colegiata de San Isidoro I,3): Dated 1162 CE.
  • MS M (Codex Mazarinaeus; Paris, Bibliothèque Mazarine 3–4): 11th/12th century.
  • MS N (Codex Bruxellensis; Brussels, Bibliothèque Royale 9107–9110): 12th century.
  • MS V (Codex Abulensis / Avila; Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional Vitr. 15–1): 12th/13th century.

B. Syriac Tradition (Versio Syra)

  • Preserved complete in the famous 6th/7th-century Peshitta codex: Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, MS B. 21 Inf. (fols. 267v–276v). Provides the most faithful and reliable oriental translation of the lost Greek text.
  • Critical Editions:
  • Ceriani, Antonio Maria. Liber IV Esdrae syriace. Monumenta Sacra et Profana 5. Milan, 1868, pp. 41–111; and photolithographic edition, MSP 6, 1876.
  • Bidawid, R. J. "4 Esdras." In The Old Testament in Syriac according to the Peshitta Version, Part IV, fasc. 3. Leiden: Brill, 1973.

C. Greek Citations and Fragments

  • Patristic Citations: Clement of Alexandria, Stromata 1.22.149.3 and 3.16.100.3; Apostolic Constitutions 2.14.9 and 8.7.6.
  • Papyrus Fragment: Oxyrhynchus Papyrus VII 1010 (4th cent. CE, containing 4 Ezra 15–16 [= 6 Ezra]).
  • Palimpsest Fragment: Rubinkiewicz, Ryszard. "Un fragment grec du IV$^e$ Livre d’Esdras (chapitres XI et XII)." Le Muséon 89 (1976): 75–89 [Preserves Greek text of the Eagle Vision in codex Marcianus Gr. 498].
  • Denis, Albert-Marie. Fragmenta pseudepigraphorum quae supersunt graeca. PVTG 3. Leiden: Brill, 1970, pp. 130–132.

D. Ethiopic (Ge'ez) Tradition

  • Preserved in over 20 manuscripts (e.g., Tānāsee 51; Paris BnF éth. 50; British Library Add. 16188).
  • Laurence, Richard. Primi Ezrae libri, qui apud Vulgatam appellatur quartus, versio Aethiopica. Oxford: Parker, 1820 [Editio princeps].
  • Dillmann, August. Veteris Testamenti Aethiopici. V. Libri apocryphi. Berlin: Asher, 1894, pp. 152–193 [Critical edition].

E. Arabic Traditions

Two distinct Arabic translations made from different Vorlagen:

  • Arabic 1 (Arab. 1): Oxford, Bodleian MS Huntington 254 (translated from the Syriac; ed. Ewald 1863).
  • Arabic 2 (Arab. 2): Mount Sinai Arabic MS 589 (9th/10th cent. CE; ed. A. Drint, CSCO 563–564, 1997).

F. Georgian Tradition

  • Preserved in the Jerusalem Georgian MS 43 (11th/12th cent.) and Mount Athos Iviron MS (10th cent.).
  • Edition: Blake, Robert P. "The Georgian Version of Fourth Esdras from the Jerusalem Manuscript." Harvard Theological Review 19 (1926): 299–375; and "from the Athos Manuscript," HTR 22 (1929): 57–105.

G. Armenian Tradition

  • Preserved in 28 biblical manuscripts, representing a highly paraphrastic, interpretive (targumic) recension.
  • Editions:
  • Yovsēp'ianc', Sahak. Ankanon girk' Hin Ktakaranac'. Venice: San Lazzaro, 1896, pp. 251–299.
  • Stone, Michael E. The Armenian Version of IV Ezra. University of Pennsylvania Armenian Texts and Studies 1. Missoula: Scholars Press, 1979.
  • Stone, Michael E. A Textual Commentary on the Armenian Version of IV Ezra. SBLSCS 34. Atlanta: Scholars Press, 1990.

H. Coptic Tradition

  • Sahidic papyrus fragment preserving 4 Ezra 13:29–48.
  • Edition: Leipoldt, Johannes, and Bruno Violet. "Ein saidisches Bruchstück des vierten Esrabuches." Zeitschrift für Ägyptische Sprache und Altertumskunde 41 (1904): 138–140.
