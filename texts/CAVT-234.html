2. Overview & Textual Contents

2 Baruch (the Syriac Apocalypse of Baruch) is a premier Jewish apocalypse composed in Judea around the end of the 1st century or early 2nd century CE (ca. 95–110 CE), in the tragic aftermath of the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. Written in a Hebrew or Aramaic original that was translated into Greek, the complete text has survived only in a single Syriac manuscript (along with an Arabic version discovered in 1975).

The work is organized into seven distinct narrative sections structured around fasting, divine dialogues, prayer, and symbolic visions:

1. The Destruction of Jerusalem (Chapters 1–12): God announces the city's destruction; angels bury the sacred Temple vessels in the earth; the Chaldeans/Romans conquer the city.

2. First Dialogue and Fast (Chapters 13–20): Baruch laments the plight of the righteous; God explains the transitoriness of this aeon (‘olam hazzeh) and the glory of the coming age (‘olam habba’).

3. Second Dialogue and the Messianic Woes (Chapters 21–30): Baruch questions the duration of history; God reveals the twelve periods of tribulation and the arrival of the Messiah, characterized by supernatural agricultural fertility.

4. Third Dialogue and the Vision of the Forest, Vine, Cedar, and Fountain (Chapters 31–43): The allegory of the four world empires (Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome); the Roman cedar is uprooted by the Messianic fountain and burned by the Vine.

5. Fourth Dialogue, Consolation, and the Nature of the Resurrection Body (Chapters 44–52): Baruch instructs the elders; God reveals that the dead will rise with their physical bodily defects so they can be recognized, and will subsequently be transformed into starry glory or hideous torment.

6. The Great Vision of the Cloud with Twelve Alternating Black and Bright Waters (Chapters 53–76): An apocalyptic survey of world history from Adam to the eschatological Prince of Peace, interpreted by the angel Ramiel.

7. The Epistle of Baruch to the Nine and a Half Tribes in Exile (Chapters 78–87): Sent by an eagle across the Euphrates, exhorting the diaspora to unyielding fidelity to the Torah until the final judgment.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

A. The Syriac Tradition

  • The Complete Apocalypse:
  • Milan Codex B. 21 inf.: Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, MS B. 21 inf., fols. 257a–267a (6th/7th c. complete Peshitta Old Testament codex) — the sole surviving witness to the complete work. Discovered and published by Antonio Maria Ceriani (1866 Latin, 1871 text, 1876–1883 facsimile).
  • Jacobite Lectionary Fragments: Four liturgical excerpts from the 12th–14th centuries (W. Baars, VT 13 [1963]: 476–478).
  • The Epistle of Baruch (Chapters 78–87):
  • Circulated widely as an independent canonical or deuterocanonical epistle in the Syriac Bible, preserved in thirty-six known manuscripts (e.g. London BL Add. 12178, Add. 14482; Paris syr. 9; Vatican syr. 268; Pococke 391).

B. The Greek Tradition

  • P. Oxy. 403: London, British Library, Papyrus 403 (4th/5th c.; discovered at Oxyrhynchus), preserving 2 Baruch 12:1–13:2 (verso) and 13:11–14:3 (recto). Edited by B. P. Grenfell and A. S. Hunt (POxy III, 1902, 4–7).
  • Patristic Citations: Reconstructed in A.-M. Denis, PVTG 3 (Leiden: Brill, 1970), 118–120; and Concordance grecque (1987), 905.

C. The Latin Patristic Quotation

  • Quoted by Cyprian of Carthage, Testimoniorum libri III ad Quirinum 3.29 (quoting 2 Baruch 48:36: “Et fient omnia nutu tuo, et quomodo dicis sic erit...”).

D. The Arabic Tradition

  • Mount Sinai Arabic MS 589: Discovered at St. Catherine's Monastery by P. S. van Koningsveld (1975); contains a 10th-century Arabic translation of the Apocalypse (without the Epistle), made directly from a Syriac exemplar. Edited critically with parallel translation by F. Leemhuis, A. F. J. Klijn, and G. J. H. van Gelder (1986).
