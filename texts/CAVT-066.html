2. Overview & Textual Contents

2 Enoch (The Book of the Holy Secrets of Enoch, or Slavonic Enoch) is an ancient pseudepigraphic apocalypse preserved exclusively in Old Church Slavonic manuscripts. Composed originally in Greek (likely based on Semitic traditions or a Semitic Vorlage) in the late 1st century CE in Hellenistic Jewish circles (often situated in Alexandria, Egypt), the work is divided into four main sections:

1. Enoch’s Heavenly Ascent (chs. 1–21): Enoch is awakened by two radiant angels and taken on a journey through the seven heavens (extended to ten heavens in the longer recension), observing celestial meteorology, astronomical bodies, fallen angels and Watchers (Grigori) imprisoned in the second and fifth heavens, paradise in the third heaven, and the angelic hierarchies praising the divine throne.

2. The Revelation of Cosmic and Primordial Secrets (chs. 22–38): Enoch stands before God, is anointed with fragrant celestial oil by Michael, transformed into a glorious angelic being, and instructed by God and the recording angel Vereveil (Pravuil) in 360 books of cosmological, astronomical, and historical mysteries, including an esoteric cosmogony featuring the primeval beings Adoil (light) and Arkhas (darkness/matter), and the creation of Adam as a microcosm (ch. 30).

3. Enoch’s Admonition of His Children on Earth (chs. 39–67): Enoch returns to earth for thirty days to deliver moral instructions, ethical testaments, and warnings against sin, idolatry, and animal cruelty to his sons (Methuselah, Regim) and his household, before his final assumption to heaven.

4. The Sacerdotal Epilogue and the Miraculous Birth of Melchizedek (chs. 68–73): The priesthood of Methuselah and his nephew Nir; the miraculous conception of Nir’s elderly, barren wife Sopanim without sexual union; her sudden death and the post-mortem birth of a fully developed infant marked with the priestly seal (hieratikon sphragida) on his chest, named Melchizedek; and his assumption by Michael to the paradise of Eden to escape the impending Deluge.

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

The text has been transmitted in over thirty known South Slavic (Bulgarian, Serbian) and East Slavic (Russian) manuscripts dating between the 14th and 18th centuries, categorized into two main recensions:

A. The Long Recension ($A$)

Characterized by expanded theological descriptions, astronomical tables, and the ten-heaven schema:

  • MS J (Sankt-Peterburg, Library of the Russian Academy of Sciences [BAN] 13.3.25; late 15th / early 16th cent. Russian manuscript; ed. Sokolov 1910).
  • MS R (Belgrade, National Library of Serbia [NLB] 321; 16th cent. Serbian manuscript; destroyed during the bombing of Belgrade in April 1941; ed. Popov 1880, Sokolov 1899/1910).
  • MS P (Moscow, State Historical Museum [GIM], Khlyudov coll. 43; 17th cent. South Russian manuscript; ed. Popov 1880, Franko 1896).

B. The Short Recension ($B$)

Characterized by a more concise narrative, the seven-heaven schema, and often considered by modern critics to reflect a less interpolated form of the translation:

  • MS U (Moscow, GIM, Uvarov coll. 3/18; 16th cent.; base text of André Vaillant’s critical edition 1952).
  • MS A (Sankt-Peterburg, BAN 45.13.4; 16th cent.; ed. Sokolov 1910).
  • MS B (Moscow, GIM, Barsov coll. 1515; 14th cent. Russian manuscript, the oldest extant manuscript; ed. Sokolov 1899/1910).
  • MS B$^2$ (Moscow, GIM, Barsov coll. 1516; 16th cent.).
  • MS N (Belgrade, NLB 151/443; 14th cent. Serbian manuscript; ed. Novaković 1884).
  • MS V (Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. Slav. 125; 16th cent.).

C. Anthological and Chronographic Witnesses
