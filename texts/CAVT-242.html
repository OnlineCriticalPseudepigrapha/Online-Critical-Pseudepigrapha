2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Apocryphon of Ezekiel is an ancient Jewish pseudepigraphon composed in Greek (or Semitic Vorlage) during the Second Temple period (1st cent. BCE to 1st cent. CE). Josephus refers to Ezekiel having left behind "two books" (Antiquitates Judaicae 10.79), pointing to an extra-canonical book circulated under Ezekiel's name alongside the canonical prophet. Although the complete text has perished, five substantial fragments survive as quotations in patristic, gnostic, and papyrological sources:

1. Fragment 1: The Parable of the Lame and the Blind (apud Epiphanius, Panarion / Adversus Haereses 64.70.5–17):

  • A wealthy king holds a royal wedding banquet for his son and invites everyone except two guards: one blind and one lame. In anger, the two infiltrate the king's garden: the blind man carries the lame man on his shoulders so the lame man can guide him and pluck the fruit. When confronted, each claims physical impossibility. The king places the lame man upon the blind man's shoulders and executes them together.
  • Theological Function: An allegory of the indivisible partnership of the physical body (sōma) and soul (psychē) in human action, establishing their joint resurrection and culpability at the Last Judgment. Directly paralleled in Rabbinic literature: b. Sanhedrin 91a–b (the dialogue between Rabbi Judah the Prince and Antoninus); Leviticus Rabbah 4.5; Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on Exod 15:1.

2. Fragment 2: The Exhortation to Repentance (apud 1 Clement 8.3; Clement of Alexandria, Paedagogus 1.10.91.2; Quis Dives Salvetur 39.2; Coptic Exegesis on the Soul, NHC II,6, pp. 135.31–136.4):

  • A prophetic call to repentance promising forgiveness even if sins reach from earth to heaven or are redder than scarlet, provided Israel returns with a whole heart and calls God "Father."

3. Fragment 3: The Miraculous Heifer (apud Clement of Alexandria, Stromata 7.16.94; Tertullian, De carne Christi 23; Epiphanius, Panarion 30.30.3; Acts of Peter 24; Gregory of Nyssa, Adversus Judaeos 3):

  • "And the heifer [sterile cow] gave birth, and did not give birth" (καὶ ἔτεκεν ἡ δάμαλις καὶ οὐκ ἔτεκεν). Adopted widely in early Christian apologetics as an ancient prophetic proof-text for the virginal conception of Mary.

4. Fragment 4: Judgment According to the Final State (apud Justin Martyr, Dialogus cum Tryphone 47.5; Clement of Alexandria, Quis Dives Salvetur 40.2; Hippolytus, De consummatione mundi 44; John Climacus, Scala Paradisi 7; Life of St. Anthony 15):

  • "In whatsoever things I find you, in these also will I judge you" (ἐν οἷς ἂν ὑμᾶς καταλάβω, ἐν τούτοις καὶ κρινῶ). A famous agraphon attributed to Ezekiel asserting that judgment falls on a person's ultimate spiritual state at death.

5. Fragment 5: The Good Shepherd and the Flocks (Chester Beatty Papyrus 20 / P. Mich. Inv. 5552):

  • 4th-century CE Greek papyrus leaf containing a poetic lament over an unfaithful, oppressive ruler/shepherd who abuses the weak and fails to seek the lost, contrasting with God's pastoral care; cf. Clement of Alexandria, Paed. 1.9.84.2–4; Origen, Homilies on Jeremiah 18.9; Manichaean Psalm-Book 239.5–6.
