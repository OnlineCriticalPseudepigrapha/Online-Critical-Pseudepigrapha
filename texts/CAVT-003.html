2. Overview & Textual Contents

The Testament of Adam (TAdam) is a short Christian pseudepigraphon from late antiquity. It was likely composed in Syriac, though it now survives in numerous languages and recensions. In many manuscripts the text is titled ”The testament of our father Adam” or something similar. The document is comprised of two main parts, usually called the Horarium and the Prophecy by modern scholars. The document is conventionally divided into four chapters, with the Horarium comprising the first two chapters and the Prophecy as chapter three. What we often call the fourth chapter is an additional section, the Hierarchy, which only appears in one manuscript and was almost certainly not part of the original document.

The Horarium

In the first main part, the Horarium, Adam lists the hours of the night and/or day in which the various creatures of the creation give praise to God, each in its appointed hour. In most manuscripts this list is divided into two sections, the "Hours of the Night" and the "Hours of the Day," each of which is allotted a chapter in the document's conventional organization. In a few manuscripts, however, one or the other of these sections is missing.

The Greek version of the Horarium seems to have a complex history. The six Greek manuscripts of TAdam contain a modified version of the Horarium that has no connection with Adam. This version is also extant in an Armenian recension. A third, simpler form of the Horarium is found in the work of George Cedrenus (11th century C.E.) where that chronicler describes revelations that Adam received from God. Cedrenus does not provide any other parts of TAdam, though his discussion of Adam's revelations may have been influenced by the Prophecy as well (see below). In any case, the relationship between these three versions of the Horarium (Syriac, Greek, and Cedrenus) is unclear.

The Prophecy

The second part of TAdam, the Prophecy, is a testament with apocalyptic features, presented as the final words of Adam recorded by his son Seth. In it Adam passes on revelations he received from God concerning the future. Some of these revelations concern the relatively near future, focusing on the Flood and Adam's own fate after death. More broadly, Adam describe the eschatological fate his posterity and the world. The Christian nature of the document is most visible when Adam predicts the divine incarnation. 

The Hierarchy

One late manuscript (Vatican Syr 164, 1702 C.E.) appends a unique section concerning the heavenly orders, with the heading ”Also from the testament of our father Adam." This section, usually designated the Hierarchy, is an angelology which lists the nine orders of the heavenly beings and their functions. Since it only appears in one manuscript, and since its content is incompatible with the narrative fiction of the two other parts, the Hierarchy was probably not part of the original TAdam. Nevertheless, modern scholars often include it as a fourth chapter of the document.

3. Date, Language & Provenance

A Syriac Christian Document

Beginning with M. E. Renan, the Testament of Adam was long regarded by modern scholars as a Gnostic work, but both Reinink and Robinson have shown this view to be wrong. Robinson has argued that the Syriac recension 1 is likely the most ancient form of TAdam known to us, and he has furnished good evidence that the original language of both the Horarium and the Prophecy was Syriac (Robinson, 137-42). If Robinson is correct, the work in its present form most likely originated within the Christian communities of Syria in late antiquity.

Earlier Jewish Sources?

The question of earlier sources is closely related to the question of unity. In all its extant forms the Horarium and the Prophecy are presented together as a unified composition, and their redactional and compositional history is difficult to untangle with any certainty. Still, the two parts are strikingly different in form and genre. Various literary elements also strongly suggest that the Horarium and the Prophecy were once two separate documents, combined and adapted at some point by a redactor to produce the Christian work we know.

The original Horarium would then likely have been a Jewish work, not necessarily related to Adam at all. The Prophecy could very well have been a Christian work from the beginning, but it could also be a heavily redacted Jewish composition. On the basis of a passage from the Apocalypse of Elijah (???), Robinson tentatively suggests that this earlier version of the Prophecy was composed in the third century C.E. and the earlier independent Horarium no later than that (Robinson, 150–3). Richard Bauckham has since made the case that the Horarium originated as a Jewish work from the second temple period (Bauckham, 2002).

The Provenance of the Hierarchy

The Hierarchy has only been superficially adapted to fit TAdam. The speaker, supposed to be Adam, refers to biblical events in past time and addresses ”my beloved” (plural; 4:1). The Hierarchy might well be a lightly redacted version of a Jewish angelology, possibly dating from the Second Temple period. The Christian additions may have involved nothing more than the interpolation of ”Jesus Christ” into 4:1 and 4:8 (see Robinson, 146-48; 159-60).

4. Manuscript Witnesses & Shelfmarks

Syriac Text

The earliest form of the Testament of Adam is witnessed in eight manuscripts dating between the 9th and the 18th centuries C.E.  The Syriac manuscripts are divided into three recensions based on content, chronology, writing system and arrangement of the four sections. (Note that the dating and alphabetic names for these manuscripts are all taken from S.E. Robinson's The Testament of Adam: An Examination of the Syriac and Greek Traditions. SBLDS 52; Chico, Ca.: Scholars Press, 1982.)

Recension 1

Recension 1 consists of the aforementioned Manuscript A (BM Add. 14,624 [folios 8b-10a]), dating to the early 9th century B.C.E., in addition to Manuscript B (Vat. Syr. 58), which is and dated to the late 16th century. Manuscript A is written in the Estrangela script with some letters in Serta, while Manuscript B is written entirely in Serta. Both manuscripts contain the Horarium (Hours of the Night and Hours of the Day) and the Prophecy in that order. In spite of the chronological differences, these two mss are nearly identical in content.

Recension 2

Recension 2 consists of Manuscript C (Vat. Syr. 164), written in Nestorian and dated to the early 18th century, and Manuscript D (BM Add 25,875), also written in Nestorian and dated to the early 18th century. Manuscript C contains the Horarium (only the Hours of the Night), the Prophecy and – as the sole witness – the Hierarchy. Manuscript D contains only the Prophecy.

Recension 3

Recension 3 consists of the following manuscripts: Manuscript E (Arund. Or. 53), written in Serta and dated to the 16th century, Manuscript F (Vat. Syr 159) written in Serta and dated to the 17th century, Manuscript G (Rylands 44) written in Nestorian and dated to the 15th century and Manuscript H (BM Add 14,577), written in Estrangela and dated to the late 9th century. All manuscripts in Recension 3 contain the Horarium except in the same order as the Greek versions, with the Hours of the Day first, followed by the Hours of the Night. Manuscripts E and H merely contain the Horarium. Manuscript F is the only one in Recension 3 that contains the Prophecy. Manuscript G also contains the so-called Treatise of Shem.

Greek Text

The Greek version of TAdam comes to us in two independent forms. Both contain only the Horarium. One recension is witnessed in six Greek manuscripts from the 15th to the 17th centuries C.E. This modified version of the Horarium bears no connection with Adam or with a testamentary context. It seems to be a late reworking of the Syriac recension 3, in part because it reverses the order of the Hours of the Night and Hours of the Day in the same way. This Greek recension bears some resemblance to late Byzantine magical texts and in most cases appears as part of a larger work on astrology and magic called Apotelesmata, attributed in some of the manuscripts to Apollonius of Tyana. This peculiar magic-Apollonius version of the Horarium is also extant in Armenian (published in Stone, pp. 39-77).

The other form of the Greek Horarium appears in one passage from Cedrenus. Here the 11th century chronicler lists the hours of the day in which prayers are sent up to heaven. Again this list is not attached to a version of the Prophecy, and it does not appear in a testamentary context. Unlike the other Greek witnesses, however, Cedrenus does connect this modified Horarium with Adam, presenting it among the revelations Adam received from God. Although Cedrenus only lists the hours of the day, the chronicler tells us that both the hours of the day and the hours of the night had been revealed to Adam. An almost verbatim passage in George Syncellus (9th century) is likely the Vorlage for Cedrenus' Horarium. Unfortunately the text of Syncellus is damaged from the point where the listing of the hours would begin.
